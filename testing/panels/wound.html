Wound panel

Bacteria (including anaerobes), fungi and viruses identified directly from wound swabs or tissue, with a reflex antibiotic-resistance gene panel.

General medical information for education only, not medical advice. Targets are listed as they appear on InnovexLab’s wound requisition form. Detection of a target does not by itself establish infection or determine treatment; results are interpreted by the ordering clinician with the clinical picture and, where indicated, culture and susceptibility testing.

When to consider testing

Wounds with clinical signs of infection: spreading erythema, warmth, swelling, purulence, increasing pain, malodor or a stalled wound. Collect after cleansing and debridement, from viable tissue where possible.

Interpretation

Chronic wounds are colonized by many organisms; detection is interpreted with the clinical exam. A resistance gene cannot be assigned to one organism in a mixed specimen.

  • Escherichia coliA normal inhabitant of the human gut and the most common cause of urinary tract infection.
  • Klebsiella pneumoniaeAn encapsulated gut organism and a major cause of healthcare-associated infection, frequently carrying resistance genes.
  • Klebsiella oxytocaA Klebsiella species found in the gut and the healthcare environment.
  • Klebsiella aerogenesA healthcare-associated Gram-negative rod, often in catheterized or ventilated patients.
  • Enterobacter cloacaeA Gram-negative rod common in healthcare settings, notable for inducible AmpC (a class C beta-lactamase) production.
  • Citrobacter freundiiA gut and environmental Gram-negative rod causing opportunistic infection.
  • Proteus mirabilisA motile, urease-producing Gram-negative rod; a cause of complicated and catheter-associated urinary infection and urinary stones.
  • Proteus vulgarisA motile, urease-producing Gram-negative rod; a cause of complicated and catheter-associated urinary infection and urinary stones.
  • Morganella morganiiA urease-producing Gram-negative rod causing opportunistic and healthcare-associated infection.
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosaA non-fermenting Gram-negative rod thriving in moist environments; a major healthcare-associated pathogen.
  • Acinetobacter baumanniiA Gram-negative coccobacillus that survives on dry surfaces and causes healthcare-associated outbreaks.
  • Kingella kingaeA Gram-negative coccobacillus that colonizes the throat of young children.
  • Bacteroides fragilis (anaerobe)An anaerobic Gram-negative rod and the most frequent anaerobe in serious infections; part of the normal colon flora.

Gram-positive bacteria (7)

  • Staphylococcus aureusA Gram-positive coccus in clusters carried on the skin and in the nose of many people, and a major cause of skin, soft-tissue and invasive infection.
  • Staphylococcus saprophyticusA coagulase-negative staphylococcus and a common cause of cystitis in young, sexually active women.
  • Enterococcus faecalisA Gram-positive coccus in pairs and short chains from the gut; hardy in the healthcare environment.
  • Enterococcus faeciumA gut enterococcus and the species most often resistant to vancomycin (VRE).
  • Clostridium perfringens (anaerobe)A spore-forming anaerobic Gram-positive rod from soil and the gut that produces tissue-damaging toxins.
  • Clostridium septicum (anaerobe)A spore-forming anaerobic Gram-positive rod from soil and the gut that produces tissue-damaging toxins.
  • Clostridium novyi (anaerobe)A spore-forming anaerobic Gram-positive rod from soil and the gut that produces tissue-damaging toxins.

  • Candida aurisAn emerging, often multidrug-resistant yeast that spreads in healthcare facilities, particularly long-term and ventilator care.
  • Candida glabrataA non-albicans Candida yeast with species-specific antifungal susceptibility; increasingly common in vaginal, urinary and invasive infection.
  • Candida kruseiA non-albicans Candida yeast with species-specific antifungal susceptibility; increasingly common in vaginal, urinary and invasive infection.
  • Candida lusitaniaeA non-albicans Candida yeast with species-specific antifungal susceptibility; increasingly common in vaginal, urinary and invasive infection.
  • Candida parapsilosisA non-albicans Candida yeast with species-specific antifungal susceptibility; increasingly common in vaginal, urinary and invasive infection.
  • Candida tropicalisA non-albicans Candida yeast with species-specific antifungal susceptibility; increasingly common in vaginal, urinary and invasive infection.
  • Fusarium solaniAn environmental mold and a cause of non-dermatophyte nail and eye infection.
  • Trichophyton interdigitaleA keratin-infecting fungus; dermatophytes as a group are the main cause of onychomycosis and tinea.
  • Aspergillus terreusAn environmental mold from soil and decaying matter that can infect nails and the ear and, with weakened immunity, the lungs.
  • Aspergillus nigerAn environmental mold from soil and decaying matter that can infect nails and the ear and, with weakened immunity, the lungs.

Viruses (5)

  • Herpes simplex virus 1A virus that establishes lifelong latent infection and reactivates, causing oral and genital herpes.
  • Herpes simplex virus 2A virus that establishes lifelong latent infection and reactivates, causing oral and genital herpes.
  • Human papillomavirus 16A high-risk virus type; types 16 and 18 cause most cervical cancers and many anal and oropharyngeal cancers.
  • Human papillomavirus 18A high-risk virus type; types 16 and 18 cause most cervical cancers and many anal and oropharyngeal cancers.
  • Varicella-zoster virus (human herpesvirus 3, HHV-3)The deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) virus that causes chickenpox and later reactivates as shingles (herpes zoster).

Reflex antibiotic-resistance genes (8)

  • AmpCClass C beta-lactamase genes that can inactivate many penicillins and cephalosporins.
  • mecA, femAMethicillin resistance in staphylococci (mecA); femA identifies Staphylococcus aureus.
  • qnrA, qnrBPlasmid-mediated genes that reduce susceptibility to fluoroquinolones.
  • vanA1, vanA2, vanBVancomycin resistance genes, found mainly in enterococci.
  • NDM, KPC, OXA-48, VIM, IMP-7Carbapenemase genes: New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase, Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase, oxacillinase-48, Verona integron-encoded metallo-beta-lactamase and imipenemase type 7.
  • SHV, TEM, CTX-M1, CTX-M2Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase gene families: sulfhydryl variable, TEM (named after a patient) and cefotaximase-Munich groups 1 and 2.
  • ermA, ermBGenes that cause resistance to macrolides such as azithromycin and erythromycin, and often to clindamycin.
  • mefAAn efflux-pump gene that causes resistance to macrolides but usually not to clindamycin.
