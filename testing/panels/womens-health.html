Women’s health panel

Organisms associated with bacterial vaginosis, vulvovaginal candidiasis and other causes of vaginitis, together with protective lactobacilli.

General medical information for education only, not medical advice. Targets are listed as they appear on InnovexLab’s general requisition form. Detection of a target does not by itself establish infection or determine treatment; results are interpreted by the ordering clinician with the clinical picture and, where indicated, culture and susceptibility testing.

When to consider testing

Vaginal symptoms with more than one possible cause: abnormal discharge, odor, itching or irritation.

Interpretation

Lactobacilli and several bacterial vaginosis (BV)-associated organisms are interpreted together as a picture of the vaginal microbiome; some organisms are normal flora.

Targets (20)

Select a target for its clinical summary, symptoms, resistance and treatment considerations.

Bacterial vaginosis-associated bacteria (5)

  • Gardnerella vaginalisA Gram-variable coccobacillus that overgrows in bacterial vaginosis when protective lactobacilli decline.
  • Atopobium (Fannyhessea) vaginaeAn anaerobic bacterium strongly associated with bacterial vaginosis.
  • BVAB2An anaerobic bacterium strongly associated with bacterial vaginosis.
  • Megasphaera type 1An anaerobic bacterium strongly associated with bacterial vaginosis.
  • Megasphaera type 2An anaerobic bacterium strongly associated with bacterial vaginosis.

Lactobacilli (3)

  • Lactobacillus crispatusA Gram-positive rod of the vaginal microbiome; L. crispatus and L. jensenii are protective, while L. iners is linked to shifts toward bacterial vaginosis.
  • Lactobacillus inersA Gram-positive rod of the vaginal microbiome; L. crispatus and L. jensenii are protective, while L. iners is linked to shifts toward bacterial vaginosis.
  • Lactobacillus jenseniiA Gram-positive rod of the vaginal microbiome; L. crispatus and L. jensenii are protective, while L. iners is linked to shifts toward bacterial vaginosis.

Other bacteria (8)

  • Enterococcus faecalisA Gram-positive coccus in pairs and short chains from the gut; hardy in the healthcare environment.
  • Escherichia coliA normal inhabitant of the human gut and the most common cause of urinary tract infection.
  • Staphylococcus aureusA Gram-positive coccus in clusters carried on the skin and in the nose of many people, and a major cause of skin, soft-tissue and invasive infection.
  • Streptococcus agalactiae (group B)A Gram-positive coccus that colonizes the gut and genital tract.
  • Neisseria gonorrhoeaeA Gram-negative diplococcus transmitted sexually and the cause of gonorrhea.
  • Mycoplasma hominisA genitourinary bacterium without a cell wall that often colonizes without causing disease.
  • Ureaplasma parvumA genitourinary bacterium without a cell wall that commonly colonizes the urogenital tract.
  • Ureaplasma urealyticumA genitourinary bacterium without a cell wall that commonly colonizes the urogenital tract.

Yeasts (4)

  • Candida albicansThe most common Candida yeast, part of normal oral, gut and vaginal flora.
  • Candida glabrataA non-albicans Candida yeast with species-specific antifungal susceptibility; increasingly common in vaginal, urinary and invasive infection.
  • Candida parapsilosisA non-albicans Candida yeast with species-specific antifungal susceptibility; increasingly common in vaginal, urinary and invasive infection.
  • Candida tropicalisA non-albicans Candida yeast with species-specific antifungal susceptibility; increasingly common in vaginal, urinary and invasive infection.
