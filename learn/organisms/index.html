Bacteria: Gram-negative (29)

  • Acinetobacter baumanniiA Gram-negative coccobacillus that survives on dry surfaces and causes healthcare-associated outbreaks.
  • Bacteroides fragilisAn anaerobic Gram-negative rod and the most frequent anaerobe in serious infections; part of the normal colon flora.
  • Bordetella speciesA small Gram-negative coccobacillus; B. pertussis causes whooping cough, and related species cause a similar illness.
  • CampylobacterA curved Gram-negative rod and a common bacterial cause of diarrhea, mainly from undercooked poultry.
  • Citrobacter freundiiA gut and environmental Gram-negative rod causing opportunistic infection.
  • Coxiella burnetiiAn intracellular bacterium from livestock and their birth products; it cannot be seen on routine Gram stain.
  • Diarrheagenic Escherichia coliA diarrhea-causing type of E. coli that acts through toxins or by attaching to or invading the intestinal lining.
  • Enterobacter cloacae complexA Gram-negative rod common in healthcare settings, notable for inducible AmpC (a class C beta-lactamase) production.
  • Escherichia coliA normal inhabitant of the human gut and the most common cause of urinary tract infection.
  • Haemophilus ducreyiA Gram-negative coccobacillus that causes chancroid, a sexually transmitted genital ulcer disease now uncommon in the United States.
  • Haemophilus influenzaeA small Gram-negative coccobacillus of the upper airway.
  • Helicobacter pyloriA curved Gram-negative rod that colonizes the stomach lining.
  • Kingella kingaeA Gram-negative coccobacillus that colonizes the throat of young children.
  • Klebsiella aerogenesA healthcare-associated Gram-negative rod, often in catheterized or ventilated patients.
  • Klebsiella oxytocaA Klebsiella species found in the gut and the healthcare environment.
  • Klebsiella pneumoniaeAn encapsulated gut organism and a major cause of healthcare-associated infection, frequently carrying resistance genes.
  • Legionella speciesA Gram-negative rod from water systems or soil and potting mix, acquired by inhaling contaminated aerosols or dust.
  • Moraxella catarrhalisA Gram-negative diplococcus of the upper airway.
  • Morganella morganiiA urease-producing Gram-negative rod causing opportunistic and healthcare-associated infection.
  • Neisseria gonorrhoeaeA Gram-negative diplococcus transmitted sexually and the cause of gonorrhea.
  • Plesiomonas shigelloidesA Gram-negative rod from fresh water and seafood.
  • Proteus mirabilis and Proteus vulgarisA motile, urease-producing Gram-negative rod; a cause of complicated and catheter-associated urinary infection and urinary stones.
  • Providencia stuartiiA Gram-negative rod strongly associated with long-term urinary catheters in older adults.
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosaA non-fermenting Gram-negative rod thriving in moist environments; a major healthcare-associated pathogen.
  • SalmonellaA Gram-negative rod acquired from contaminated food (poultry, eggs, produce) and animal contact.
  • Serratia marcescensAn environmental Gram-negative rod associated with healthcare outbreaks, catheters and contaminated solutions.
  • ShigellaA highly transmissible Gram-negative rod; a very small inoculum causes infection, so spread in group settings is common.
  • Vibrio speciesA curved Gram-negative rod from salt and brackish water, including Gulf Coast waters, and raw or undercooked shellfish.
  • Yersinia enterocoliticaA Gram-negative rod acquired mainly from undercooked pork; grows at refrigeration temperatures.

Bacteria: Gram-positive (11)

  • Clostridioides difficileA spore-forming anaerobic Gram-positive rod and a major cause of antibiotic-associated and healthcare-associated diarrhea.
  • Clostridium perfringens, C. septicum and C. novyiA spore-forming anaerobic Gram-positive rod from soil and the gut that produces tissue-damaging toxins.
  • Enterococcus faecalisA Gram-positive coccus in pairs and short chains from the gut; hardy in the healthcare environment.
  • Enterococcus faeciumA gut enterococcus and the species most often resistant to vancomycin (VRE).
  • Group A StreptococcusA beta-hemolytic Gram-positive coccus in chains; a cause of strep throat, skin and soft-tissue infection and invasive disease.
  • Group B StreptococcusA Gram-positive coccus that colonizes the gut and genital tract.
  • Group C and G StreptococcusA beta-hemolytic streptococcus that causes infections resembling group A Streptococcus, particularly in older adults with other conditions.
  • Listeria monocytogenesA Gram-positive rod from soil and foods such as deli meats, soft cheeses and smoked seafood; grows at refrigeration temperatures.
  • Staphylococcus aureusA Gram-positive coccus in clusters carried on the skin and in the nose of many people, and a major cause of skin, soft-tissue and invasive infection.
  • Staphylococcus saprophyticusA coagulase-negative staphylococcus and a common cause of cystitis in young, sexually active women.
  • Streptococcus pneumoniaeA Gram-positive lancet-shaped diplococcus colonizing the nasopharynx and the most common bacterial cause of community-acquired pneumonia.

Bacteria: atypical and cell-wall-deficient (7)

  • Chlamydia pneumoniaeAn obligate intracellular bacterium spread by respiratory droplets.
  • Chlamydia trachomatisAn obligate intracellular bacterium and the cause of the most commonly reported bacterial sexually transmitted infection in the United States.
  • Mycoplasma genitaliumA sexually transmitted bacterium without a cell wall.
  • Mycoplasma hominisA genitourinary bacterium without a cell wall that often colonizes without causing disease.
  • Mycoplasma pneumoniaeA small bacterium without a cell wall that spreads by respiratory droplets, often in households, schools and institutions.
  • Treponema pallidumA spirochete and the cause of syphilis; it does not grow in routine culture.
  • Ureaplasma urealyticum and Ureaplasma parvumA genitourinary bacterium without a cell wall that commonly colonizes the urogenital tract.

Vaginal microbiome bacteria (3)

  • Atopobium vaginae, BVAB2 and MegasphaeraAn anaerobic bacterium strongly associated with bacterial vaginosis.
  • Gardnerella vaginalisA Gram-variable coccobacillus that overgrows in bacterial vaginosis when protective lactobacilli decline.
  • Lactobacillus speciesA Gram-positive rod of the vaginal microbiome; L. crispatus and L. jensenii are protective, while L. iners is linked to shifts toward bacterial vaginosis.

Viruses: respiratory (12)

  • AdenovirusA virus with many types: some cause respiratory illness and conjunctivitis, and enteric types 40 and 41 cause gastroenteritis in children.
  • EnterovirusesOne of a large group of ribonucleic acid (RNA) viruses spread by the fecal-oral and respiratory routes, with a summer and fall peak.
  • Human bocavirusA small deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) virus found in respiratory specimens from young children, often alongside other viruses.
  • Human metapneumovirusAn RNA virus related to respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), causing late-winter and spring respiratory illness.
  • Human parainfluenza virusesAn RNA virus causing respiratory infection; types 1 and 2 cause croup in children, and type 3 causes bronchiolitis and pneumonia.
  • Human parechovirusAn RNA virus related to enteroviruses; important in young infants.
  • Influenza virusesAn RNA virus; influenza A and B cause seasonal epidemics, while influenza C usually causes mild infection.
  • Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)An RNA virus causing winter respiratory illness; serious in infants, older adults and people with heart or lung disease.
  • RhinovirusThe most frequent cause of the common cold; closely related to enteroviruses.
  • SARS-CoV and MERS-CoVA coronavirus behind severe outbreaks: severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) in 2003 and, since 2012, Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS).
  • SARS-CoV-2The coronavirus that causes coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19).
  • Seasonal human coronavirusesA common coronavirus that circulates every year; distinct from severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2).

Viruses: gastrointestinal (4)

  • AstrovirusAn RNA virus causing mild gastroenteritis, mostly in children and older adults.
  • NorovirusThe most common cause of acute gastroenteritis and of outbreaks in long-term care, cruise ships and restaurants.
  • RotavirusA major cause of severe diarrhea in young children worldwide; vaccines against rotavirus A have greatly reduced severe disease.
  • SapovirusA calicivirus related to norovirus that causes gastroenteritis and outbreaks.

Viruses: skin, eye and genital (3)

  • Herpes simplex virus (HSV-1 and HSV-2)A virus that establishes lifelong latent infection and reactivates, causing oral and genital herpes.
  • Human papillomavirus types 16 and 18A high-risk virus type; types 16 and 18 cause most cervical cancers and many anal and oropharyngeal cancers.
  • Varicella-zoster virus (human herpesvirus 3, HHV-3)The DNA virus that causes chickenpox and later reactivates as shingles (herpes zoster).

Fungi: yeasts (3)

  • Candida albicansThe most common Candida yeast, part of normal oral, gut and vaginal flora.
  • Candida aurisAn emerging, often multidrug-resistant yeast that spreads in healthcare facilities, particularly long-term and ventilator care.
  • Other Candida speciesA non-albicans Candida yeast with species-specific antifungal susceptibility; increasingly common in vaginal, urinary and invasive infection.

Fungi: molds, dermatophytes and Pneumocystis (5)

  • Alternaria, Acremonium and NeofusicoccumAn environmental mold occasionally responsible for nail and skin infection; also a frequent contaminant, so repeat detection supports a causal role.
  • Aspergillus terreus and Aspergillus nigerAn environmental mold from soil and decaying matter that can infect nails and the ear and, with weakened immunity, the lungs.
  • DermatophytesA keratin-infecting fungus; dermatophytes as a group are the main cause of onychomycosis and tinea.
  • Fusarium solaniAn environmental mold and a cause of non-dermatophyte nail and eye infection.
  • Pneumocystis jiroveciiA fungus that causes pneumonia in people with weakened immunity, such as people with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and a low CD4 count (a measure of helper T cells, a type of immune cell), transplant recipients and people on steroids or chemotherapy.

Parasites (6)

  • CryptosporidiumA chlorine-tolerant protozoan parasite spread through recreational water, contact with animals and from person to person.
  • Cyclospora cayetanensisA protozoan parasite associated with imported fresh produce and summer outbreaks.
  • Dientamoeba fragilisAn intestinal protozoan whose role in disease is debated; often found without symptoms.
  • Entamoeba histolyticaA protozoan parasite acquired from contaminated food and water, mainly in travelers and immigrants from endemic areas.
  • Giardia duodenalisA protozoan parasite spread through contaminated water and from person to person, including in child care settings.
  • Trichomonas vaginalisA sexually transmitted protozoan parasite and the cause of the most common non-viral sexually transmitted infection.
